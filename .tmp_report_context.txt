P25: ABSTRACT
P26: Manual scoring of whole-night polysomnography (PSG) is slow, costly and subjective, and automated systems that classify each 30-second epoch independently ignore the sequential structure of sleep. This report presents a temporally aware, multimodal sleep stage classification framework. Using the Sleep-EDF Expanded dataset, the system preprocesses EEG, EOG and EMG signals with wavelet denoising, per-subject normalisation, wake trimming and 30-second epoching; extracts 116 time-domain, frequency-domain, Hjorth, permutation entropy and TKEO features; and selects 67 of them with an ensemble method that combines LightGBM importance, mutual information and permutation importance with stability scoring, modality weighting and correlation filtering. Baseline LightGBM and ExtraTrees classifiers are trained with SMOTE and undersampling under a leakage-free subject-wise protocol, and a temporal layer (HMM with Viterbi decoding, linear-chain CRF and window BiLSTM) exploits the sequence of epochs. In preliminary results, HMM/Viterbi improves the baseline from 0.696 to 0.724 macro F1 and from 0.426 to 0.439 N1 F1, while N1 remains the main challenge.

P113: INTRODUCTION
P115: 1.1 Introduction to Sleep and Sleep Staging
P116: Sleep is a recurring, physiologically active state that is essential for memory consolidation, metabolic regulation, immune function and emotional well-being. Disturbed or insufficient sleep is associated with a wide range of chronic physiological and psychosocial conditions, and interrupted or poor-quality sleep has been linked to many chronic physiological and psychosocial illnesses. Because sleep is not a uniform state but a structured sequence of distinct stages, assessing its quality requires more than measuring how long a person sleeps. Clinicians need to know how much time is spent in each stage, how quickly the person falls asleep, how often sleep is interrupted, and how the stages are organised across the night.
P117: The clinical gold standard for such an assessment is polysomnography (PSG). A PSG study is an all-night recording of several physiological signals at once. The signals relevant to sleep staging are the electroencephalogram (EEG), which measures cortical brain activity; the electrooculogram (EOG), which captures eye movements; and the electromyogram (EMG), usually recorded from the chin, which measures muscle tone. Together, these three modalities carry complementary information: EEG describes the brain state, EOG reveals the rapid eye movements that characterise REM sleep, and EMG reveals the muscle atonia that accompanies REM sleep and the raised muscle tone seen during wakefulness.
P118: Sleep staging is the task of labelling consecutive 30-second segments of a PSG recording, called epochs, with one of the sleep stages defined by a scoring standard. The older Rechtschaffen and Kales (R&K) standard has been largely replaced by the manual of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), which defines five stages: Wake (W), three non-REM stages (N1, N2 and N3) and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. Table 1.1 summarises the defining characteristics of each stage. The resulting sequence of stages over the night is called a hypnogram, and from it clinicians derive summary measures such as total sleep time, sleep efficiency, sleep onset latency and the percentage of the night spent in each stage.
P119: Table 1.1: AASM sleep stages and their characteristic signal patterns
P121: The reliability of any staging depends on how consistently these patterns are recognised. For N2 and N3 the patterns are distinctive: spindles and K-complexes mark N2, and the slow, high-amplitude waves of N3 are difficult to miss. N1, in contrast, has no single hallmark pattern. It is a brief, transitional stage in which the EEG gradually slows from the alpha rhythm of relaxed wakefulness to the theta activity of light sleep, and its features overlap with those of both Wake and N2. This difficulty of N1 is a recurring theme of this report.
P122: 1.2 Motivation
P123: Manual scoring of a full-night PSG recording is carried out by trained technicians or clinicians who inspect the signals epoch by epoch. A single night contains roughly one thousand 30-second epochs, and scoring them is slow, costly and tedious. It is also subjective: it is widely recognised that two experienced scorers rarely agree on every epoch, and that agreement is lowest for the transitional stage N1. This combination of workload and variability is a significant bottleneck in sleep medicine and limits access to sleep diagnostics.
P124: Automatic sleep staging with machine learning offers a way to reduce this bottleneck, and a large body of work has been published on the subject. Recent research has increasingly favoured deep neural networks, which learn features directly from raw signals. While often accurate, these models are costly to train, and their internal reasoning is difficult for a clinician to inspect. Studies have also shown that carefully designed pipelines built on handcrafted features and conventional classifiers can match deep learning approaches while remaining transparent and cheaper to deploy. This observation motivates the feature-based approach adopted in this project.
P125: Despite this progress, three limitations of existing automated systems motivate the present work:
P126: •	Epoch-independent classification. Most feature-based systems classify each 30-second epoch in isolation, ignoring the fact that sleep is a continuous process in which stages occur in runs and follow typical transitions. Discarding this sequential context hurts accuracy most on ambiguous epochs, and particularly on N1.
P127: •	Weak performance on N1. The transitional stage N1 has no single hallmark pattern, and it is the stage on which automated systems, including deep networks, most often struggle. Context from neighbouring epochs is especially valuable here, because an ambiguous N1 epoch is easier to interpret when its neighbours are known.
P128: •	Optimistic evaluation. When epochs from the same night are split at random between training and test sets, near-identical epochs appear on both sides and the measured accuracy is inflated. Evaluation by subject is needed to estimate performance on people the model has never seen.
P129: 1.3 Problem Statement
P130: Existing automated sleep staging systems typically classify each 30-second epoch independently, ignoring the sequential nature of sleep, which limits accuracy on the hardest stage, N1. Their performance is also often measured with evaluation protocols that do not separate subjects, which can give optimistic estimates. The problem addressed by this project is to design and evaluate a multimodal, feature-based sleep staging pipeline that models temporal context across epochs and is evaluated under a strict subject-wise protocol that prevents data leakage.
P131: 1.4 Objectives
P132: The objectives of the project are:
P133: 1.	To build a reproducible preprocessing pipeline for multimodal PSG recordings (two EEG channels, EOG and EMG) from the Sleep-EDF Expanded dataset.
P134: 2.	To engineer a rich set of time-domain, frequency-domain and non-linear features, and to reduce them to a compact, non-redundant subset using an ensemble feature selection method.
P135: 3.	To train and evaluate baseline classifiers with class-imbalance handling under a strict subject-wise protocol that prevents data leakage.
P136: 4.	To add a temporal modeling layer and to measure its effect on overall performance and especially on N1-stage classification.

P140: CHAPTER - 2
P141: LITERATURE REVIEW
P143: This chapter reviews prior work relevant to the project. It follows the development of automatic sleep staging from feature-based machine learning, through deep learning, to the more recent concerns of temporal context, explainability and generalisation, and it ends with a summary of the research gap that this project addresses.
P144: 2.1 Manual Scoring Standards
P145: Sleep staging began as a manual procedure. The R&K standard divided non-REM sleep into four stages (S1 to S4), plus wake, REM and a movement category. The AASM manual later merged stages 3 and 4 into a single slow-wave stage N3, renamed S1 and S2 to N1 and N2, and standardised the 30-second epoch, channel placement and scoring rules. The Sleep-EDF Expanded dataset used in this project was annotated according to R&K, so a label mapping to the five AASM classes is required, as described in Chapter 5. Both standards rely on a human reading signals epoch by epoch, which is the source of the time and variability problems discussed in Section 1.2.
P146: 2.2 Feature-Based Machine Learning Approaches
P147: Early automatic systems extracted handcrafted features from each epoch and passed them to a conventional classifier. Typical features include statistical descriptors in the time domain, band powers in the standard EEG frequency bands, Hjorth parameters and entropy measures. Satapathy et al. applied ReliefF to select the most informative features from EEG, EOG and EMG signals and then used an AdaBoost classifier, showing that multimodal signals combined with feature selection give a competitive result using a relatively simple model. Xu et al. combined multi-domain EEG and EOG features with a two-phase feature selection scheme and an XGBoost classifier, reporting an accuracy of 87.0%. Notably, their results are described as being especially significant because deep learning models had struggled on the N1 stage.
P148: Van Der Donckt et al. made the case for this family of methods in a systematic comparison, concluding that simple and interpretable techniques can be competitive with deep learning for sleep scoring. These studies, together with interpretable feature-based pipelines in related biosignal tasks, show that a well-constructed pipeline of feature extraction, feature selection and a strong ensemble classifier can reach accuracy comparable to end-to-end networks. They also have practical advantages: they are cheap to train and deploy, they do not require very large datasets, and their inputs are meaningful physiological quantities, which makes explanation possible.
P149: 2.3 Deep Learning Approaches
P150: Deep learning methods have become popular because they learn features automatically from raw signals or time-frequency images. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been applied to single-channel and multichannel EEG. Tsinalis et al. used stacked sparse autoencoders on time-frequency features and convolutional networks on single-channel EEG. Supratak et al. proposed DeepSleepNet, which combines a CNN for epoch-level feature learning with a bidirectional LSTM that models transitions between epochs. Recurrent networks, Transformer-based models such as SleepTransformer, and hybrids have followed. Chengfan et al. compared CNN, LSTM and CNN+LSTM models on EEG spectrograms, with the best configuration reaching 87.4% accuracy, and Jia et al. proposed GraphSleepNet, which models the spatial and temporal relationships among EEG channels as a graph.
P151: These models often reach high accuracy, but the cost is growing model complexity: model size and computational cost have increased considerably without proportionate gains in accuracy. More importantly for clinical use, they behave as black boxes whose decisions cannot easily be traced back to signal features. That limitation is the main reason that this project follows the feature-based route.
P152: 2.4 Temporal Context in Sleep Staging
P153: Sleep stages occur in runs and follow typical transitions: wake is followed by N1, N1 by N2, N2 by N3 or REM, and a stage rarely lasts a single epoch. Several lines of work exploit this structure. Deep models such as DeepSleepNet and the mixed neural network of Dong et al. include recurrent layers that carry information across epochs. Cascaded LSTM models and attention-based models pursue the same idea. In the feature-based setting, temporal structure can be introduced by post-processing, for example with a hidden Markov model (HMM): the stages are treated as hidden states, the classifier output serves as the observation, and Viterbi decoding finds the most likely stage sequence.
P154: Conditional random fields (CRFs) are a discriminative alternative to HMMs that model dependencies between neighbouring labels directly, and bidirectional LSTMs read a window of epochs in both directions. These three families — HMM/Viterbi, CRF and windowed BiLSTM — are the temporal models compared in this project.
P155: 2.5 Explainability in Sleep Staging
P156: Explainable AI methods attribute a prediction to the input features that influenced it. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) assigns each feature a contribution to a prediction based on cooperative game theory and has efficient exact algorithms for tree ensembles, which makes it a natural match for LightGBM-based pipelines. Within sleep staging, recent work such as SleepExplain explains NREM and REM stage classification from EEG using attribution methods, and Vilamala et al. examined what convolutional networks learn when scoring sleep. Xu et al. emphasised that interpretable features are central to an effective and interpretable staging system. Most existing studies, however, explain model behaviour on aggregate; per-epoch attributions that are carried through to a patient-level narrative are rare.
P157: 2.6 Cross-Dataset Generalisation
P158: A model trained on one dataset commonly loses accuracy on another, because of differences in recording hardware, electrode montage, sampling rate, scoring practice and patient population. Domain adaptation is one response: Tallal et al. proposed STDA-Net, a spectrogram-based domain adaptation method for cross-dataset sleep stage classification. Federated learning, studied by Amato on ISRUC-Sleep data, addresses heterogeneity between sites from a different angle. ISRUC-Sleep, described by Khalighi et al., is a comprehensive public dataset that includes both healthy subjects and subjects with sleep disorders, which makes it a widely used resource for studying how sleep staging generalises across populations. Per-subject normalisation, used in this project, is a simple and widely applicable step that removes much of the between-subject amplitude variability.
P159: 2.7 Large Language Models for Clinical Reporting
P160: Large language models can turn structured numerical data into fluent text. In the reference study, quantitative sleep metrics — total sleep time, sleep efficiency, sleep latency and stage percentages — were assembled into a structured prompt for the Gemini 1.5 Flash model, which produced a report with an overall summary, a sleep score, an analysis of sleep architecture and recommendations. The quality of the generated report was checked in two ways: a second LLM acting as a judge evaluated factual consistency against the input data, and BERTScore measured semantic similarity with a ground-truth summary. 
P161: 2.8 Research Gap
P162: The review shows that feature-based pipelines can match deep networks while remaining interpretable, and that temporal context is usually introduced through deep recurrent models. The pattern is visible in the studies reviewed above: many studies classify each epoch on its own, rely on black-box models, or evaluate on a single dataset. Few studies combine an interpretable multimodal feature pipeline with an explicit temporal layer whose effect on the N1 stage is measured under a strict subject-wise protocol. This project addresses that gap: it keeps the interpretable multimodal feature pipeline, adds a temporal layer aimed at the N1 stage, and evaluates the result without data leakage.

P173: CHAPTER - 4
P174: SYSTEM DESIGN
P176: This chapter presents the design of the system. It gives the high-level design, which shows how the modules fit together, and the low-level design of the individual modules, each with a description.
P177: 4.1 High-Level Design
P178: Figure 4.1 shows the architecture of the system as a sequence of ten blocks, from the input dataset to the outputs. The first four blocks bring the data into a form suitable for learning, blocks five to seven select features and train the epoch-wise classifiers under a subject-wise protocol, and blocks eight and nine add the temporal modeling layer and evaluate the results. Each block is described below.
P180: Figure 4.1: High-level architecture of the system.
P181: 1.	Sleep-EDF Expanded. The input dataset, with the Sleep Cassette (SC) and Sleep Telemetry (ST) subsets. Each recording consists of a PSG file and a hypnogram file in EDF format.
P182: 2.	Signal loading. The recordings are read with MNE-Python and PyEDFlib. Four channels are used: EEG Fpz-Cz, EEG Pz-Oz, horizontal EOG and submental EMG.
P183: 3.	Preprocessing. Annotations are mapped to the five classes W, N1, N2, N3 and REM, the signals are resampled to 100 Hz and denoised with a db4 wavelet, each channel is standardised with a per-subject Z-score, the signals are cut into 30-second epochs, and excess wake is trimmed.
P184: 4.	Feature dataset. Every epoch becomes one row that carries the subject ID, record ID, epoch index and stage label, together with 116 candidate features.
P185: 5.	Subject-wise evaluation. Subjects are kept separate across the training and test sets, which prevents epoch-level data leakage.
P186: 6.	Ensemble feature selection. LightGBM gain and mutual information, permutation importance with a stability score, modality weights and correlation filtering retain up to 67 features.
P187: 7.	Epoch-wise classifiers. LightGBM and ExtraTrees are trained with class weights or with SMOTE and random undersampling (SMOTE-RUS), and produce five-stage probability estimates for every epoch.
P188: 8.	Temporal modeling. Out-of-fold probabilities are refined with HMM/Viterbi smoothing, a linear-chain CRF and a window BiLSTM.
P189: 9.	Evaluation. Accuracy, macro F1 and Cohen's Kappa are reported, together with per-class precision, recall and F1, with emphasis on the N1 F1 score, and confusion matrices.
P190: 10.	Explainable outputs. The list of selected features is an output of the pipeline. SHAP attributions and an evidence-grounded clinical-style report are part of the future scope described in Section 8.2.
P191: Data move through the system as a sequence of progressively more compact representations. A recording enters as four continuous signals sampled at a fixed rate together with a list of annotated stage intervals. After preprocessing, each recording is represented as a set of 30-second epochs, where each epoch contains one segment per channel and is paired with an integer stage label and a subject identifier. The subject identifier is carried through the entire pipeline because it is needed to split the data without leakage. After feature extraction, an epoch becomes a vector of 116 numbers, and after selection a vector of 67 numbers; the epochs of a recording, kept in their original order, form a table whose rows are consecutive in time. This preserved order is what makes the temporal modeling layer possible. The output of the classifier is a probability for each of the five stages for every epoch, and the output of the temporal layer is a refined label sequence for the whole night.
P192: 4.2 Low-Level Design
P193: 4.2.1 Signal preprocessing module
P194: Figure 4.2 shows the stages of the preprocessing module. A recording and its hypnogram are loaded and the four channels are resampled to a common sampling rate of 100 Hz. Wavelet denoising then removes high-frequency noise, and each channel is normalised with a Z-score computed within a single subject's recording. Annotations are mapped to the five classes, long stretches of wake at the start and end of the night are trimmed, and the continuous signal is cut into non-overlapping 30-second epochs. The order of these stages is chosen so that the signal-level operations (resampling, denoising and normalisation) are completed before the data are split into epochs and labelled.
P196: Figure 4.2: Low-level design of the preprocessing module.
P197: 4.2.2 Feature extraction module
P198: The feature extraction module (see Figure 4.3) computes five families of features from each epoch: time-domain statistics, frequency-domain band powers, Hjorth parameters, permutation entropy, and Teager–Kaiser energy operator (TKEO) features. Time-domain statistics and band powers describe the waveform and spectrum of the signals, Hjorth parameters and permutation entropy characterise the statistical and dynamic complexity of the EEG, and TKEO features are computed on the EOG and EMG channels, where they highlight brief bursts of eye movement and muscle activity. All features of an epoch are concatenated into a single vector of 116 values, and each feature keeps a name that records its channel and family, so that it can be traced back to its physiological meaning during explanation.
P200: Figure 4.3: Low-level design of the feature extraction module.
P201: 4.2.3 Feature selection module
P202: Not all of the 116 features are equally useful, and many are strongly correlated with each other. The feature selection module (see Figure 4.4) therefore scores every feature in three complementary ways — LightGBM importance, mutual information with the stage label, and permutation importance — and combines these scores. A stability score rewards features that rank highly consistently across validation folds, and a modality weighting gives priority to features from EEG over those from EOG and EMG, reflecting the central role of EEG in the scoring rules. Finally, a correlation filter removes features that are nearly duplicates of an already retained feature. The result is a compact subset of 67 features that is both predictive and minimally redundant.
P204: Figure 4.4: Low-level design of the ensemble feature selection module.
P205: 4.2.4 Baseline classification module
P206: The classification module trains gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM) and extremely randomised trees (ExtraTrees) on the selected features. Because the stages are very unevenly represented (Wake and N2 are far more common than N1 and N3), the training data are balanced by synthetic oversampling of minority stages (SMOTE) combined with random undersampling of majority stages. The models are tuned, and a modality ablation retrains them on EEG-only, EOG-only, EMG-only and combined feature sets to measure the contribution of each signal. The module outputs a probability for each stage for every epoch, which is also the input to the temporal layer.
P207: 4.2.5 Temporal modeling module
P208: The baseline treats each epoch separately. The temporal module (see Figure 4.5) takes the sequence of stage probabilities of a whole night and refines it using the structure of sleep. Three alternatives are implemented and compared. The HMM with Viterbi decoding treats the stages as hidden states with learned transition probabilities and finds the most probable overall stage sequence. The linear-chain CRF models the dependence between neighbouring labels directly. The window BiLSTM reads a window of consecutive epochs in both directions and predicts the stage of the epoch in focus. All three produce a smoothed hypnogram, so they can be compared on identical terms.
P210: Figure 4.5: Low-level design of the temporal modeling module, showing the three alternatives compared.

P227: 5.3 Signal Preprocessing
P228: 5.3.1 Resampling
P229: Recordings from different sources and channels do not all use the same sampling rate. To obtain uniform epochs, every channel is resampled to a common rate of 100 Hz. A common rate means that every 30-second epoch has the same number of samples in every channel and every recording, which allows epochs to be stacked into arrays and feature extractors to be applied uniformly.
P230: 5.3.2 Wavelet denoising
P231: Physiological signals are contaminated by noise from electrical interference, electrode movement and muscle activity. Conventional fixed filters are less effective for EEG, which is non-stationary, because a filter that removes noise at one moment can also remove genuine signal at another. Wavelet denoising adapts to the signal. It has three steps: the signal is decomposed into several frequency levels with a discrete wavelet transform; coefficients whose magnitude is below a statistically derived threshold are set to zero or shrunk towards zero (soft thresholding); and the signal is reconstructed from the modified coefficients. In soft thresholding, a coefficient w is replaced by
P232: 	ŵ = sign(w) · max(|w| − λ, 0)	(1)
P233: where λ is the threshold. Fine-scale coefficients dominated by noise are suppressed, while larger coefficients that carry the sleep-relevant waveform are kept. The wavelet family used is Daubechies 4 (db4), and the number of decomposition levels is [FILL: number of levels].

For the EEG Pz-Oz channel the original trace is dominated by fast, jagged fluctuations around a slower waveform; after denoising, these are strongly reduced while the slow waveform is retained. For the EEG Fpz-Cz channel and the EOG channel the two curves almost coincide, which shows that the large-scale waveform, including the sharp peaks, is preserved when little noise is present. The EMG trace is already smooth, and denoising softens a few of its peaks slightly. Overall, the figure confirms that noise is removed while the main structure of the signal is kept.

P237: 5.3.3 Per-subject Z-score normalisation
P238: The amplitude of EEG, EOG and EMG signals varies greatly between people because of differences in skull thickness, skin impedance and electrode placement. These differences are unrelated to sleep stage. To remove them, each channel of each subject's recording is standardised using that recording's own mean and standard deviation:
P239: 	z = (x − μsubject,channel) / σsubject,channel	(2)
P240: The statistics are computed from a single individual's data only. As a result, the classifier sees signals on a common scale and is encouraged to learn patterns that are shared across people instead of person-specific baselines, which improves its ability to generalise to unseen subjects.
P241: 5.3.4 Wake trimming and epoch segmentation
P242: Sleep laboratory recordings often contain long periods of wakefulness before lights-out and after the final awakening. These periods are not informative and, if kept, make Wake by far the dominant class. Excess wake periods at the beginning and end of each recording are therefore trimmed [FILL: how much wake is retained, for example 30 minutes before and after sleep]. The continuous signals are then cut into non-overlapping 30-second epochs, the standard unit of the AASM scoring manual, and each epoch is given the label of the annotation that covers it.
P243: 5.4 Feature Extraction
P244: Each epoch is converted into a vector of 116 features computed from the four channels. The features fall into five families, described in Table 5.3. Time-domain and frequency-domain features are standard in sleep analysis, while Hjorth parameters, permutation entropy and the TKEO add information about signal complexity and transient events.
P245: Table 5.3: Feature families extracted from each epoch
P247: Frequency-domain features. The power spectral density of each epoch is estimated, for example with Welch's method, and the power in each canonical band is integrated. Relative band power divides each band's power by the total power, which makes it robust to amplitude scaling. Slow delta power is the signature of N3, sleep spindles raise power in the sigma band during N2, and the alpha rhythm is the signature of relaxed wakefulness.
P248: Hjorth parameters. For a signal x(t), Hjorth mobility and complexity are defined from the variances of the signal and its first and second derivatives:
P249: 	Mobility = √( var(x′) / var(x) ),   Complexity = Mobility(x′) / Mobility(x)	(3)
P250: Mobility is proportional to the mean frequency of the signal and complexity measures how similar the signal is to a pure sine wave, so together they describe the statistical and spectral character of the EEG with just two numbers.
P251: Permutation entropy. The signal is divided into short vectors, and each vector is replaced by the order pattern of its values. The Shannon entropy of the distribution of these patterns measures how regular or complex the signal is. Entropy tends to be lower in the regular slow-wave activity of deep sleep and higher in the irregular activity of wakefulness and REM.
P252: Teager–Kaiser energy operator. For a discrete signal x[n], the operator is
P253: 	ψ(x[n]) = x[n]² − x[n−1] · x[n+1]	(4)
P254: It tracks the instantaneous energy of a signal and responds strongly to short bursts, which makes it suited to detecting the rapid eye movements of REM sleep in the EOG and the brief muscle activity in the EMG. [FILL: summary statistics computed from the TKEO]

P256: 5.5 Feature Selection
P257: Using all 116 features increases training cost and the risk of overfitting, and many features carry almost the same information. An ensemble selection procedure reduces the set to 67 features in four stages.
P258: 1.	Three importance measures. Each feature is scored by (i) the importance assigned by a LightGBM model, (ii) its mutual information with the stage label, and (iii) its permutation importance, which is the drop in model performance when the feature's values are shuffled. The three measures have different strengths and biases, so combining them is more reliable than relying on one.
P259: 2.	Stability scoring. The scores are computed across several validation folds, and a stability score rewards features whose rank stays high from fold to fold. This avoids selecting features that look important only in one particular split.
P260: 3.	Modality weighting. The combined score is multiplied by a weight that reflects domain knowledge: features from EEG are favoured over those from EOG and EMG, because EEG is the primary basis of the scoring rules. [FILL: weights used for EEG, EOG and EMG]
P261: 4.	Correlation filtering. When two retained features are highly correlated (above a threshold [FILL: correlation threshold]), the lower-ranked one is removed. This leaves a subset that is predictive and minimally redundant.
P262: The outcome of the four stages is a set of 67 features, which is used by all later modules. [CONFIRM: that feature scoring and selection were performed using training subjects only]
P263: 5.6 Baseline Classification
P264: 5.6.1 Classifiers
P265: Two ensemble classifiers are used as baselines. LightGBM is a gradient boosting framework that builds decision trees sequentially, with each new tree correcting the errors of the previous ones; it is fast, handles tabular features well and provides feature importance. ExtraTrees builds many randomised decision trees independently and averages their votes, which reduces variance. Both are well suited to a moderately sized, tabular feature set and require no feature scaling. Their hyperparameters were tuned [FILL: tuned hyperparameters and search method, for example number of trees, depth, learning rate].
P266: 5.6.2 Handling class imbalance
P267: The class distribution is strongly skewed (see Figure 7.1 in Chapter 7): N2 and Wake dominate while N1 and N3 are rare. A classifier trained on such data tends to favour the common stages. To counter this, the training data are rebalanced with SMOTE, which creates synthetic examples of minority classes by interpolating between neighbouring real examples, combined with random undersampling of the majority classes. The resampling is applied only to the training data, never to the evaluation subjects, so the evaluation reflects the real class distribution.
P268: 5.6.3 Subject-wise evaluation
P269: The evaluation protocol is designed to prevent information from the evaluation subjects reaching the model during training. All Sleep-EDF subjects are divided into training subjects and held-out subjects, and every epoch of a subject goes to exactly one of the two groups. Models are fitted and class balancing is applied using training subjects only, and held-out subjects are used only to compute metrics. This matters because consecutive epochs from one person are highly similar: if epochs were split at random, near-identical epochs from the same night would fall on both sides of the split and the measured accuracy would be inflated. Since some subjects contributed more than one night, the split is made by the subject identifier extracted in Section 5.2 and not by recording. [FILL: split scheme, for example number of folds or train/test subject counts]
P270: 5.6.4 Modality ablation
P271: To measure how much each signal contributes, the classifiers are retrained using only EEG features, only EOG features, only EMG features, and all features together. The comparison shows whether the multimodal combination is better than any single modality, and which stages depend on which signal; the results are reported in Chapter 7.
P272: 5.7 Temporal Modeling
P273: The baseline classifier outputs, for each epoch, a probability for each of the five stages. The temporal models take the whole sequence of these outputs for a night and refine it.
P274: 5.7.1 Hidden Markov Model with Viterbi decoding
P275: In the HMM formulation, the five stages are hidden states, and the transition matrix A, with entries aij = P(stage j at epoch t+1 | stage i at epoch t), is estimated from the training hypnograms. It captures the facts that stages are persistent and that some transitions (for example W to N1) are far more common than others (for example W to N3). The classifier's output plays the role of the observation. The most probable state sequence is found with the Viterbi algorithm, whose recursion is
P276: 	δt(j) = maxi [ δt−1(i) · aij ] · bj(ot)	(5)
P277: where bj(ot) is the emission score of stage j for the observation at epoch t, and the best path is recovered by back-tracking. Because a single epoch that disagrees with both of its neighbours is unlikely under the transition matrix, isolated errors are smoothed out. [CONFIRM: how emission scores are derived from the classifier output and how the transition matrix is estimated]
P278: 5.7.2 Linear-chain Conditional Random Field
P279: A linear-chain CRF is a discriminative sequence model. Given the observation sequence x, the probability of a label sequence y is
P280: 	P(y | x) = (1/Z(x)) · exp( Σt Σk λk fk(yt−1, yt, x, t) )	(6)
P281: where fk are feature functions on label transitions and observations, λk their learned weights, and Z(x) a normalising constant. Unlike the HMM, the CRF learns the weights of transition and observation features jointly. [CONFIRM: features supplied to the CRF and training settings]
P282: 5.7.3 Window BiLSTM
P283: A long short-term memory network processes a sequence while maintaining a memory cell, and a bidirectional LSTM reads the sequence both forwards and backwards, so each position is informed by both past and future context. In this module, a window of consecutive epochs is fed to the BiLSTM and the network predicts the stage of the epoch in focus. [FILL: window length, hidden size, number of layers, loss function, optimiser, epochs]
P284: 5.8 Evaluation Metrics
P285: The following metrics are computed from the confusion matrix. For each class c, with true positives TP, false positives FP and false negatives FN,
P286: 	Precision = TP / (TP + FP),   Recall = TP / (TP + FN),   F1 = 2 · Precision · Recall / (Precision + Recall)	(7)
P287: Accuracy is the fraction of epochs classified correctly. Macro F1 is the unweighted mean of the five per-class F1 scores, so each stage counts equally regardless of how common it is. Cohen's Kappa measures agreement beyond what would be expected by chance:
P288: 	κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe)	(8)
P289: where po is the observed agreement (equal to accuracy) and pe is the agreement expected by chance from the class frequencies. Kappa is widely used in sleep staging because it is the standard way to report agreement between scorers.
P291: CHAPTER - 6
P292: SYSTEM TESTING
P294: Testing of a machine learning pipeline differs from testing conventional software. In addition to checking that each module runs and produces correctly shaped output, one has to check that the data are processed faithfully, that the evaluation is free of leakage, and that the reported numbers can be reproduced. This chapter describes the testing strategy, the validation that has been carried out, and the test cases defined for the system.
P295: 6.1 Testing Strategy
P296: Testing is organised at four levels. Data-level tests check that recordings are paired correctly with annotations, that labels are mapped correctly, and that epochs have the expected shape. Module-level tests check each processing stage on its own, for example that the denoising removes noise without distorting the signal and that the feature extractor returns the expected number of features. Protocol-level tests check that the evaluation is leakage-free, which is the most important property for the trustworthiness of the results. Result-level tests check that reported metrics are consistent with the underlying predictions.
P297: 6.2 Validation of Signal Preprocessing
P298: The denoising stage was validated by visual inspection of the original and denoised signals for each channel, as shown in Figure 5.1. The check confirmed three expected properties: noise is clearly reduced on the noisiest channel (EEG Pz-Oz); channels with little noise (EEG Fpz-Cz and EOG) are left essentially unchanged; and sharp peaks that carry sleep-relevant information are preserved. The EMG trace showed slight softening of a few peaks, which is acceptable because the features derived from EMG describe muscle tone level and short bursts rather than the exact peak amplitudes.
P299: 6.3 Validation of the Evaluation Protocol
P300: The evaluation protocol (Section 5.6.3) separates training and evaluation by subject. The principal protocol-level checks are that no subject identifier occurs in both groups, that class balancing by SMOTE and undersampling is applied to training data only, and that any statistic learned from data, such as the normalisation used for a subject, is computed from that subject's data only. These checks matter because a random epoch-wise split would let near-identical epochs from the same night appear on both sides of the split, and would inflate the measured accuracy. This is one reason why the results reported in Chapter 7 are lower than values reported for epoch-wise splits in the literature.
P301: 6.4 Reproducing the Reported Metrics
P302: A result-level test was carried out to confirm that the headline metrics are consistent with the stored predictions. The accuracy, macro F1 and Cohen's Kappa of the baseline and of the HMM/Viterbi model were recomputed from their confusion matrices (reported in Section 7.4) and compared with the values produced by the experiment scripts. Table 6.1 shows that the recomputed values agree with the reported values to four decimal places, which confirms that the confusion matrices and the reported scores describe the same set of predictions.

P360: 7.9 Discussion
P361: Taken together, the results support three conclusions. First, temporal context helps: a simple HMM on top of an epoch-independent classifier raises accuracy, macro F1 and Kappa under a strict subject-wise protocol. Second, the benefit is uneven. Stages that occur in long runs (REM, N2 and Wake) gain the most, while the brief and ambiguous N1 stage gains little, and its recall actually decreases. Third, a more flexible temporal model is not automatically better: the CRF and BiLSTM, in this first run, are worse than the baseline, which shows that the temporal layer needs careful treatment of class imbalance and tuning.
P362: Table 7.7 supports the second of these conclusions by listing the six largest error flows. The temporal layer reduces four of them: N2 labelled as N1 falls by about 30%, N2 labelled as REM by 25%, W labelled as N1 by 19% and N2 labelled as N3 by 16%. The two flows in which a true N1 or N3 epoch is labelled N2, however, grow by 19% and 10%. This indicates that smoothing pulls short or boundary epochs towards the dominant neighbouring stage, N2, which explains why the temporal layer helps stable stages more than N1 and N3.

P367: CHAPTER - 8
P368: CONCLUSION AND FUTURE SCOPE
P370: 8.1 Conclusion
P371: This project set out to build the foundation of a temporally aware, multimodal sleep staging system, and the pipeline has been implemented and evaluated end to end on the full Sleep-EDF Expanded dataset. Raw recordings are denoised, normalised per subject, trimmed and segmented into 30-second epochs. A set of 116 time-domain, frequency-domain, Hjorth, permutation entropy and TKEO features is extracted from the EEG, EOG and EMG channels, and an ensemble selection method that combines LightGBM importance, mutual information and permutation importance with stability scoring, modality weighting and correlation filtering reduces it to 67 features. Baseline classifiers are trained under a subject-wise protocol with SMOTE and undersampling.
P372: The temporal modeling comparison gave a clear first result. Under the strict subject-wise protocol, the baseline reaches a macro F1 of 0.696 and an N1 F1 of 0.426. An HMM with Viterbi decoding improves these to 0.724 and 0.439, with accuracy rising from 0.758 to 0.791 and Kappa from 0.672 to 0.713. The confusion matrices show that the gain comes mainly from REM, N2 and Wake, while N1 remains the main challenge: it is mostly confused with N2, and the temporal layer trades some N1 recall for precision. The CRF and BiLSTM did not outperform the baseline in this first run and require further tuning.
P373: The work therefore delivers a transparent, reproducible and leakage-free foundation: stage predictions, class probabilities and named features on which the extensions described in Section 8.2 can be built.
P374: 8.2 Future Scope
P375: The work can be extended in four directions: improving the N1 stage and completing the full-dataset experiments, explaining predictions, generating evidence-based sleep reports, and validating the system on a second dataset.
P376: 8.2.1 Improving N1 classification and completing the experiments
P377: 1.	Improve N1 classification. Apply class weighting or focal loss in the CRF and BiLSTM, tune the HMM transition strength so that short N1 episodes are preserved, examine the BiLSTM window length, and add features aimed at N1, such as slow rolling eye movements in the EOG and the shift from alpha to theta activity in the EEG.
P378: 2.	Complete the full-dataset experiments. Tune all models and compare them on macro F1, Kappa and N1 F1, and test the differences for statistical significance across subjects.
P379: 8.2.2 SHAP-based explainability
P380: SHAP assigns to each feature i a contribution, the Shapley value, computed as the weighted average of its marginal contribution over all subsets S of the other features:
P381: 	φi = ΣS ⊆ F∖i [ |S|! (|F| − |S| − 1)! / |F|! ] · [ f(S ∪ i) − f(S) ]	(9)
P382: For tree ensembles such as LightGBM, TreeSHAP computes these values exactly and efficiently. For each epoch, the features with the largest contribution towards the predicted stage will be extracted and expressed in physiological terms, for example high relative delta power supporting N3 or low EMG energy supporting REM. Attributions will also be aggregated by stage to show which features characterise each stage overall.
P383: 8.2.3 LLM-based sleep report generation
P384: From the predicted hypnogram, the module will compute total sleep time, sleep efficiency (the percentage of time in bed spent asleep), sleep onset latency, wake after sleep onset and the percentage of time in each stage. These metrics, together with the per-stage SHAP evidence, form a structured summary that is placed in a prompt asking the language model to act as a sleep specialist and write a report with a summary, a sleep score, an analysis of sleep architecture against normal ranges, the supporting evidence and recommendations. The prompt will instruct the model to use only the numbers and evidence supplied.
P385: 8.2.4 Cross-dataset validation on ISRUC-Sleep
P386: The trained pipeline will be applied unchanged to ISRUC-Sleep, a public dataset of 126 recordings from healthy subjects and subjects with sleep difficulties. The ISRUC channels will be mapped to the closest equivalents of the Sleep-EDF channels, and the same preprocessing, per-subject normalisation, feature extraction and selected feature subset will be used, with nothing re-fitted on ISRUC. The drop in macro F1, N1 F1 and Kappa relative to Sleep-EDF will quantify generalisation, and the confusion matrices will show which stages are most sensitive to the change of dataset. If a drop is observed, simple remedies such as calibration on a few subjects or domain adaptation will be studied.
P387: 8.2.5 Towards clinical use
P388: In the longer term, the system should be compared with the scores of several human scorers, studied on recordings from patients with sleep disorders, and offered through a lightweight interface through which a clinician can inspect the hypnogram, the explanations and the report together.
